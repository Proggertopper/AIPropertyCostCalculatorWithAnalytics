Inspection and Repair Requests : Seller Negotiation Playbook

The home inspection is where many deals get tense—and where net proceeds can swing by thousands. Buyers often use inspection reports to ask for repairs, credits, or price reductions. Sellers worry about being “nickel-and-dimed,” while buyers worry about hidden costs. The goal is not to “win” the argument. The goal is to close the deal while protecting your net proceeds and avoiding unnecessary repairs that delay closing or invite future liability.

This guide shows exactly how inspection negotiations work, what requests are reasonable, when to offer a credit vs do repairs, how to handle financing-required repairs, and how to respond with calm, structured language that keeps the deal moving.

Quick Answer

Most sellers should respond to inspection repair requests with a triage approach: fix safety issues and financing blockers, consider credits for true defects that would reasonably change price, and refuse cosmetic “wish list” requests or normal wear-and-tear items—especially if the home was priced to condition.

In many cases, offering a repair credit is better than doing the repairs because it avoids delays, contractor scheduling risk, and the possibility the buyer dislikes the workmanship. Repairs are most valuable when they (1) prevent the deal from failing, (2) keep financing on track, or (3) eliminate a major buyer fear that would tank demand if the home returns to market.

Seller rule: Don’t negotiate from emotion. Negotiate from net proceeds and closing probability. The best offer is the one that maximizes expected net: net proceeds × probability of closing.

How Inspection Negotiations Work (Seller Perspective)

A home inspection is typically the buyer’s opportunity to evaluate the property’s condition during the contract period. The inspector produces a report that may include dozens of items—from serious safety hazards to minor maintenance notes. Buyers then decide whether to request:

  • Repairs (you fix items before closing)
  • Repair credits (you give money back at closing, usually as a credit)
  • Price reduction (sale price decreases)
  • No change (buyer accepts condition)
  • Cancel (if contingency allows)

Sellers sometimes feel insulted by the list, but it’s often a standard negotiation move: buyers ask for more than they expect to receive. Your job is to respond strategically and calmly.

Why buyers ask for credits even when the house is “fine”

From the buyer’s view, inspection is the moment they can reduce uncertainty and reduce cost. Even if the home is generally solid, buyers often try to: (1) lower total out-of-pocket costs, (2) build a “repair reserve,” and (3) feel protected from surprises. This doesn’t mean they think your home is bad—it means they are bargaining when they have leverage.

Inspection Contingency Timeline (What Sellers Should Watch)

Contract timelines vary by state and deal terms, but the structure is similar: the buyer schedules inspections within a defined window and must submit repair requests by a deadline. Then the seller responds, and both sides either agree, renegotiate, or exit.

Seller priorities during the timeline

  • Control speed: delays create buyer doubt and increase deal failure risk.
  • Track deadlines: missed deadlines can change leverage.
  • Keep documentation: estimates, receipts, and clear written agreements.
  • Prevent re-trades: some buyers “re-trade” late—ask again at appraisal or final walk-through.

Practical move: Ask for the buyer’s “top 5” items. Many lists are padding. Focusing negotiations on key issues reduces time and conflict.

What Buyers Usually Ask For (Common Repair Requests)

Inspection reports can be long and intimidating. Many items are minor. Common categories:

Major / structural / water issues

  • Roof leaks, missing shingles, failing flashing
  • Foundation cracking or evidence of movement
  • Drainage issues, standing water, grading problems
  • Basement/crawlspace moisture, mold concerns

Systems and safety

  • Electrical hazards (open junction boxes, wiring issues)
  • Plumbing leaks, water heater issues
  • HVAC safety or functionality
  • Gas leaks or venting concerns

Maintenance and “normal wear” items

  • Loose outlets, missing GFCI in some areas
  • Small plumbing drips
  • Caulk/grout, minor wood rot
  • Sticky doors/windows, minor drywall cracks

Cosmetic / preference requests

  • Paint, flooring upgrades
  • Appliance replacements (when working)
  • “Update” requests (not defects)

The negotiation usually hinges on the first two categories. The rest is where sellers should be selective.

Triage Framework: Fix vs Credit vs Refuse

The fastest way to respond is to triage every request into one of three buckets. This makes your response rational, not reactive.

Bucket 1: Fix it (usually)

Fix items that are:

  • Safety hazards (electrical, gas, major trip hazards, etc.)
  • Active leaks or urgent water intrusion
  • Financing blockers likely to stop the loan from closing
  • Simple repairs that are cheap and remove a big objection

These repairs protect the deal. They also protect you if the buyer cancels and you must relist, because the next buyer will find the same issues.

Bucket 2: Credit (often best)

Offer a credit when:

  • Repairs would delay closing (contractors not available)
  • There’s uncertainty about scope (buyer wants “replace all plumbing” based on one leak)
  • You want the buyer to control the contractor and finish quality
  • The item is legitimate but you don’t want work crews in the house

Credits are also useful when buyers request multiple small repairs. Bundling into a single credit can be cheaper than handling 10 separate repairs.

Bucket 3: Refuse (reasonable boundaries)

Refuse requests that are:

  • Purely cosmetic upgrades (new paint, new floors)
  • Normal wear and tear items that were visible during the showing
  • Overreach (replace a system that works because it’s “old”)
  • Requests that contradict “as-is” pricing or disclosed condition

Boundary rule: You are not obligated to deliver a “new home.” You are obligated to deliver the home in the contracted condition, subject to agreed repairs/credits and truthfulness in disclosures.

Repair Credits Explained (How They Work at Closing)

A repair credit is usually a seller concession applied at closing, often reducing the buyer’s cash needed to close (or covering specific closing costs), depending on how the credit is structured and lender rules. In practice, credits are the “cleanest” way to resolve repair disputes quickly.

Why sellers like credits

  • Speed: no waiting on contractors
  • Less liability: you’re not guaranteeing workmanship
  • Simplicity: one line item instead of multiple repair tasks

Why buyers like credits

  • They choose contractors and timing
  • They can prioritize fixes after moving in
  • They avoid the risk of rushed repairs before closing

Potential downsides of credits

Credits may be limited by loan rules or may not be as “cash-like” as buyers expect. Some buyers ask for credits hoping to reduce their cash-to-close. If credit structure is constrained, you may need to negotiate differently (price reduction or repairs).

Seller tip: Always translate a credit request into net proceeds. A $5,000 credit is the same as $5,000 less net to you—unless it prevents a much larger price cut or deal failure.

When Doing Repairs Is Better Than Offering a Credit

Credits are often efficient, but repairs can be the best option in certain cases:

1) When the issue is a financing blocker

If a lender or appraiser requires an item to be repaired (handrails, roof issues, safety hazards), offering a credit won’t solve the closing problem. The repair must be done.

2) When the repair is simple and cheap

If a licensed contractor can fix an issue quickly for a small amount, doing the repair can remove friction. Examples: a minor plumbing leak, a missing GFCI, a loose stair rail, a small roof patch (depending on scope).

3) When you suspect the buyer will overstate costs

Some buyers present inflated estimates. If you can fix the issue for less than the credit request, repairs may protect net. (But don’t create delays that risk the deal.)

4) When the repair will help even if the deal fails

If the deal fails, you relist. Fixing a major defect improves the home’s position for the next buyer. Sometimes “repair now” is not about this buyer—it’s about marketability.

Financing and Appraisal “Required Repairs”

Many sellers learn too late that “inspection repairs” and “lender-required repairs” are different. Even if a buyer is willing to accept condition, the lender may not.

Common lender/underwriting concerns (varies by loan)

  • Roof condition (active leaks, end-of-life concerns)
  • Safety hazards (exposed wiring, missing rails)
  • Non-functioning major systems
  • Water damage or mold concerns
  • Structural issues

How sellers should manage this risk

  • Prefer buyers with strong financing and clear pre-approval
  • Ask for proof of funds or strong underwriting (when appropriate)
  • Be realistic: some homes are better suited for cash buyers if condition is poor
  • Fix obvious safety hazards before listing (reduces surprise)

Negotiation Strategy That Protects Net and Keeps the Deal Alive

The inspection phase is a negotiation. Strategy matters more than arguing line-by-line. Here is the seller playbook approach:

1) Anchor to pricing and disclosure

If the home was priced to reflect age/condition, remind the buyer that the price already reflects visible condition. You can acknowledge legitimate defects while refusing “upgrade requests.”

2) Ask for the buyer’s priorities

Many repair lists contain low-priority items. Ask for “top 5” concerns so you can focus on real deal-breakers.

3) Use documentation to control the numbers

Don’t negotiate off vague claims. Ask for contractor estimates or invoices. If you counter with your own estimate, you shift negotiation from emotion to evidence.

4) Offer a clean solution

Bundling multiple items into one credit can keep the deal moving. Buyers often prefer simplicity too, as long as they feel heard on major defects.

5) Consider the cost of losing the deal

Sellers sometimes refuse a reasonable credit and then lose weeks, pay holding costs, and later accept a lower price. The right comparison is: “What does this concession cost versus the expected cost of relisting?”

Seller mindset: The inspection phase is about removing deal risk at the lowest cost. You are buying certainty when you concede reasonably on true defects.

Seller Scripts (Copy/Paste Responses)

Script A: “As-is pricing” boundary + reasonable concession

“Thank you for the inspection report. The home was priced to reflect its age and visible condition, and we’re not planning to address cosmetic items or upgrades. We are willing to resolve the following safety-related and true defect items: [list]. For the remaining items, we can offer a credit of $___ in lieu of repairs to keep closing on schedule.”

Script B: Request documentation

“We’re open to addressing major defects. Please provide contractor estimates for the items you consider highest priority, and we’ll respond with a repair or credit proposal based on documented costs.”

Script C: Offer repairs for financing blockers only

“We’re willing to complete repairs that are required for financing and safety. For non-required items, we’re offering a credit of $___ to maintain timeline and avoid delaying closing.”

Script D: Decline with professionalism

“We reviewed the requests carefully. We will not be addressing cosmetic items or normal wear-and-tear that was visible during showings. Our response focuses on true defects and safety items to keep the transaction moving.”

Deal Risk: When to Concede, When to Counter, When to Walk

Inspection negotiations can fail when the buyer’s expectations are unrealistic or when the home has bigger issues than either side anticipated. Use these signals:

Signs the buyer is reasonable

  • Focuses on major defects, not small cosmetics
  • Provides estimates and stays within documented ranges
  • Wants to keep timeline intact

Signs the buyer may “re-trade” later

  • Asks for broad, vague credits without documentation
  • Continues adding new items after agreement
  • Threatens to cancel repeatedly to gain leverage

Walking away can be rational if the buyer is using inspection as a tool to get an entirely different deal than the one agreed to. But before you walk, compare to the cost of relisting and the likelihood you’ll face the same defect objections with the next buyer.

Pre-Listing Inspection: Is It Worth It?

Some sellers do a pre-list inspection to identify issues early. This can:

  • Reduce surprise negotiation later
  • Allow targeted repairs that improve marketability
  • Help you price accurately to condition

The downside is you may learn issues you must disclose (rules vary), and you may pay for an inspection without a guaranteed payoff. Pre-inspections are often most useful for older homes, homes with visible risk (water, roof, electrical), or when you want a clean sale.

Common Seller Mistakes During Inspection Negotiations

1) Treating every request as an insult

Buyers ask for more than they expect. Respond with structure, not emotion.

2) Doing repairs without controlling scope

If you agree to “fix plumbing,” define exactly what is being repaired. Vague repair promises create conflict later.

3) Using unlicensed contractors or low-quality rushed work

Rushed work can fail final walk-through or create disputes. If you repair, use appropriate professionals and keep receipts.

4) Overconceding because you’re afraid to lose the deal

Concessions should be tied to major defects and net proceeds math. Overconceding reduces net unnecessarily.

5) Refusing reasonable concessions and then paying more later

A small credit now can avoid weeks of holding costs and a lower final sale price later. Compare scenarios.

Seller Checklist: Inspection and Repair Requests

  • ✅ Read the full report and identify major defects vs minor notes
  • ✅ Ask buyer for “top priority” items
  • ✅ Triage: fix vs credit vs refuse
  • ✅ Request documentation / estimates for major items
  • ✅ Check financing risk: are any items loan blockers?
  • ✅ Make a clean written response with specific scope
  • ✅ If repairing: hire appropriate pros and keep receipts
  • ✅ If crediting: confirm structure and closing feasibility
  • ✅ Track deadlines and keep momentum
  • ✅ Re-evaluate if buyer shows re-trade behavior

Fast Stress Tests (Protect Your Net)

1) “Credit vs lose the deal” test

Compare a credit amount to the expected cost of relisting: additional holding costs, potential price cut, and lost momentum. If losing the deal is likely to cost more, a reasonable credit can be rational.

2) “Repair delay” test

If a repair will delay closing, what does that cost in mortgage interest, taxes, and utilities? A credit may be cheaper and safer.

3) “Next buyer” test

If this buyer cancels, will the next buyer raise the same issue? If yes, fixing now may be better than arguing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should the seller fix repairs after a home inspection?

Sometimes. Most sellers prioritize safety issues and financing blockers, then consider credits for true defects. Cosmetic “wish list” items and normal wear-and-tear are often refused, especially if the home was priced to condition.

What should sellers refuse to repair?

Sellers commonly refuse cosmetic upgrades, maintenance notes that are normal for the home’s age, and requests to replace working systems just because they’re older. The key is to remain reasonable on true defects while refusing overreach.

Is it better to offer a repair credit or do the repairs?

Credits are often better when repairs could delay closing or create workmanship disputes. Repairs are better when they are required for financing or when a simple fix prevents deal failure. Compare cost, time, and closing risk.

How much should a seller credit after inspection?

Use documentation and focus on major defects. Ask for estimates, counter with realistic numbers, and compare the cost of a concession to the cost of losing the deal and relisting. There’s no universal number because markets and defects vary.

Bottom Line

Inspection repair requests are normal. A smart seller response is structured: prioritize safety and financing blockers, use credits to keep timelines intact, and refuse cosmetic wish lists. Negotiate based on documented defects and net proceeds—not emotion. The goal is to remove deal risk at the lowest cost while protecting your net.
