How to Appeal a Property Tax Assessment

If your property tax bill jumped, the reason is often the assessed value (or a lost exemption), not a mysterious “rate change.” The good news: most counties let homeowners challenge an assessment—especially when the value is above fair market value or the property record is wrong. This guide walks you through the appeal process step-by-step: what evidence wins, how to pick strong comps, what deadlines matter, and how to avoid the common mistakes that kill appeals.

Quick answer

You appeal property taxes by appealing the assessed value (or correcting the property record). The winning strategy is evidence-based: show that your assessment is above fair market value or based on incorrect facts. Most appeals fail because homeowners miss the deadline or use weak comps.

A practical way to think about it:

  • You’re not arguing “taxes are unfair.” You’re arguing “the assessed value is too high” (or wrong).
  • Your job is to prove a lower value with recent comparable sales + documented differences.
  • Deadlines are strict. If you miss the window, you typically wait until next year.

This is general educational content. Property tax appeal rules are local; always confirm deadlines and forms on your county assessor/appraiser website.

How assessments work (what you’re really appealing)

Your annual property tax bill usually starts with a value: the county’s estimate of what your property is worth (or a taxable version of that value). Then a local rate is applied, exemptions are subtracted, and the final bill is calculated.

Simple model:
Property tax bill ≈ (Taxable assessed value) × (Local tax rate)

When you appeal, you’re usually trying to lower the taxable assessed value by doing one (or more) of the following:

  • Prove market value is lower than the assessor’s estimate (using comps and adjustments).
  • Correct factual errors in the property record (square footage, beds/baths, lot size, basement/garage, condition, quality grade).
  • Document negative factors that reduce value (deferred maintenance, functional obsolescence, external issues like noise or proximity).
  • Confirm exemptions/classification (homestead, senior, disability, agricultural) are applied correctly.

Assessment vs appraisal vs sale price

It’s common to see different “values”:

  • Market value: what a willing buyer would pay (based on comps).
  • Assessed value: the county’s value used for taxes (often derived from market value but not always equal).
  • Purchase price: what you paid (a strong data point, but not always decisive if the market shifted or the sale had unique factors).

Some jurisdictions assess near market value annually; others reassess on a cycle. Some cap annual increases for primary residences. These structural differences change the “best appeal argument.” But most appeals still come back to the same question: Is the value correct relative to comparable sales?

When it’s worth appealing (and when it’s not)

You should appeal when there’s a clear mismatch between the assessed value and reality—and when the potential savings justify the effort. A property tax appeal is often worth it when:

Good reasons to appeal

  • The assessment jumped after purchase and appears above what similar homes sell for.
  • Your property record is wrong (square footage, baths, finished basement, extra features you don’t have).
  • Your home has condition issues that comps don’t (roof, foundation, significant deferred maintenance).
  • Comparable homes are assessed lower despite being similar (sometimes a uniformity/equity argument).

Situations where appealing may be a waste of time

  • Your assessed value is already below market (common in fast-appreciating markets).
  • Your comps aren’t truly comparable (different neighborhood, school zone, lot size, condition, or major layout differences).
  • The potential savings are small relative to your time and stress.

Quick test: If you can’t find at least 3 strong comps suggesting a lower value, your appeal is weak. Start with comps before you start paperwork.

Deadlines and timelines (the #1 reason appeals fail)

Most jurisdictions have a short appeal window after assessments are mailed. It might be 15, 30, 45, or 60 days—sometimes longer—but it’s rarely unlimited. Miss the deadline and you typically wait until next year.

Common timeline structure

  1. Assessment notice issued (value notice mailed or posted online)
  2. Informal review period (optional; call/email meeting with assessor)
  3. Formal appeal deadline (file with a board/tribunal)
  4. Hearing scheduled (you submit evidence by a deadline)
  5. Decision issued (value adjusted or upheld)

The most important action step is simple: find your county’s appeal deadline, put it on your calendar, and aim to submit early. If you’re collecting comps or contractor estimates, you want buffer time.

Pro tip: Even if you start with an informal review, don’t let it distract you from the formal deadline. If the informal route fails, you still need time to file formally.

Best evidence for a property tax appeal

Property tax appeals are won with evidence, not emotion. The strongest appeals typically combine: (1) great comps, (2) corrected facts, and (3) documentation of condition issues where relevant.

1) Comparable sales (“comps”) — the most persuasive evidence

A strong comp is:

  • Close by (same neighborhood or very similar competing area)
  • Recent (as close to the valuation date as possible)
  • Similar in size, lot, age, layout, condition, and features
  • Arms-length (a normal sale, not a family transfer)

Most homeowners lose because their comps aren’t actually comparable. If your comp is 40% larger, has a renovated kitchen, or is in a different school district, the board may ignore it.

How many comps do you need?

Aim for 3–6 strong comps. More is not always better if the extra comps are weak. You want a tight set of “this is what buyers pay for homes like mine.”

2) Property record errors — the easiest “slam dunk” wins

Before you even pick comps, pull your assessor’s property record card (or online details) and verify:

  • Living area (square footage)
  • Bedrooms/bathrooms
  • Basement finished/unfinished status
  • Garage type and size
  • Lot size
  • Year built, additions, and remodel claims
  • Quality grade/class and condition rating

If the county thinks you have a finished basement but it’s unfinished, that matters. If the square footage is wrong, that matters. These errors can significantly inflate value and are often easier to prove than market arguments.

3) Condition issues — when your home is objectively worse than comps

Condition is a legitimate value driver. Boards tend to respond best to concrete evidence:

  • Photos (dated, labeled, showing the issue clearly)
  • Professional estimates (roof replacement, foundation repair, HVAC replacement)
  • Inspection report excerpts (if relevant and not too long)
  • Permits showing unfinished work or open violations (if applicable)

Important: “My kitchen is old” is weak. “My roof is failing and here is a written estimate” is strong.

4) Equity/uniformity arguments (advanced)

Some jurisdictions allow appeals based on uniformity: similar properties should be assessed similarly. This can be powerful if you can show your assessment is high relative to near-identical homes. But it’s more technical and may require careful selection of “assessment comps,” not just sales comps.

How to appeal your property tax assessment (step-by-step)

Step 1: Confirm the valuation date and what you’re appealing

Assessments are based on a valuation date (sometimes January 1, sometimes another date). You want comps and evidence that align with that date. Also confirm whether you’re appealing: assessed value, market value, classification, exemption, or a factual error.

Step 2: Pull your property record and look for errors

This is the highest ROI first step. If the record is wrong, you can often win quickly. Print/save the record page as part of your evidence packet.

Step 3: Build a comp set (3–6 strong comps)

Use sold listings (MLS data via agent, public records, reputable real estate sites) and focus on similarity. Create a simple table for each comp:

  • Address (or parcel ID)
  • Sale date and sale price
  • Living area, beds/baths, lot size
  • Condition notes (renovated vs dated)
  • Key differences vs your home

Step 4: Make a reasonable value conclusion

Many homeowners aim too low and lose credibility. A conservative, evidence-based number often performs better. Think: “If I were a buyer, what would I pay given these comps and differences?”

Step 5: Try an informal review (optional, but often worth it)

Many counties allow an informal review with the assessor before a formal hearing. If you have obvious record errors or strong comps, an informal review can result in a correction without a hearing. But again: do not miss the formal filing deadline while you pursue informal review.

Step 6: File the formal appeal by the deadline

File the official form, include required documents, and keep proof of submission. Some boards require you to submit evidence in advance; others allow evidence at the hearing. Follow local rules carefully.

Step 7: Prepare a one-page “argument summary”

Winning appeals are easy to understand. Your summary should include:

  • The assessed value you’re challenging
  • Your requested value
  • 3–6 comps with sale prices
  • Key differences and adjustments (plain English)
  • Record errors and condition evidence (photos/estimates)

Step 8: Attend the hearing and stick to the evidence

Be calm, organized, and brief. Your goal is to demonstrate that the current value is not supported by market evidence. Avoid emotional arguments (“taxes are too high,” “I can’t afford it”) and focus on value.

Hearing tips: what to say (and what not to say)

Say this (strong)

  • “My assessed value is above fair market value. Here are 4 comparable sales near the valuation date.”
  • “The property record is incorrect: it lists a finished basement, but it’s unfinished. Here are photos.”
  • “This comp is most similar because it matches size and condition; the other comp is larger and renovated.”
  • “Given these comps and differences, a reasonable value is $X.”

Don’t say this (weak)

  • “My taxes are too high.”
  • “My neighbor pays less” (without showing comparable facts and values)
  • “The market is bad” (without comps tied to the valuation date)
  • “I found cheaper homes somewhere else” (different neighborhood, school district, or property type)

Best mindset: Treat it like a mini-appraisal discussion. You’re presenting the evidence for a reasonable market value.

Common mistakes that ruin property tax appeals

1) Missing the deadline

This is the #1 reason appeals fail. Put the deadline on your calendar the day you receive the notice. Aim to submit early.

2) Using weak comps

Comps must compete with your home. Different area, different school district, different condition, or too old a sale = weak. Strong comps are better than many comps.

3) Asking for an unrealistic value

If you ask for a value that’s far below evidence, you lose credibility. Conservative, defensible numbers win more often than “hail Mary” numbers.

4) Not correcting property record errors first

Many homeowners jump straight into comp research and miss that the county record is wrong. Fixing errors can be the easiest win.

5) Confusing exemptions with assessment value

Sometimes the issue is not value—it’s a missing homestead exemption or wrong property classification. Always check exemptions before building a full market-value case.

DIY templates (copy/paste)

Template 1: informal review email to assessor

Subject: Request for informal review of assessed value — [Property Address / Parcel ID]

Hello [Assessor/Appraiser Office],

I’m requesting an informal review of the assessed value for my property at [address], parcel ID [ID].
The current assessed value is [$____]. Based on recent comparable sales near the valuation date and the property’s condition/record details, I believe fair market value is closer to [$____].

Attached are: (1) a one-page summary, (2) 3–6 comparable sales, and (3) documentation of [record error/condition issue] (photos/estimate).

Please let me know the next steps and whether additional documentation is needed.

Thank you,
[Name]
[Phone]

Template 2: one-paragraph appeal statement

I appeal the assessed value of my property at [address/parcel] for the [tax year] assessment. The assessed value of [$____] exceeds fair market value as of the valuation date. Recent comparable sales of similar homes in the same market area support a lower value. Additionally, the property record contains the following inaccuracies: [list]. Based on the attached comparable sales and documentation, a reasonable value is [$____].

Adjust these templates to your county’s specific forms and requirements.

Property tax appeal checklist

  • ✅ Find the appeal deadline and valuation date
  • ✅ Pull your property record card and verify facts
  • ✅ Identify 3–6 strong comps (similar, nearby, recent)
  • ✅ Document condition issues with photos + estimates (if applicable)
  • ✅ Write a one-page summary with your requested value
  • ✅ Attempt informal review (optional) without missing the formal deadline
  • ✅ File the formal appeal with proof of submission
  • ✅ Prepare for hearing: evidence packet + calm presentation

Best single lever: comps that are truly comparable. If you get comps right, everything else becomes easier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you appeal your property tax assessment and lower your taxes?

Yes. Most counties allow an informal review and/or a formal appeal if you believe the assessed value is incorrect. The strongest cases are supported by comparable sales, corrected property facts, and condition documentation.

What evidence works best?

The best evidence is recent comparable sales near the valuation date, plus documentation of differences (size, condition, features). Factual errors and serious condition issues supported by photos and written estimates often strengthen the case.

Will appealing raise my taxes instead?

In many jurisdictions, the appeal is focused on your requested reduction. But procedures vary. If you’re concerned about risk, review your local rules and consider asking the assessor how the process works before submitting. Most homeowners appeal only when evidence strongly supports a lower value.

Should I hire a tax appeal company?

If potential savings are large (high-value property or high-tax area) or the case is complex, professional help may be worth it. Many firms charge a contingency fee (a share of savings). For smaller cases, a DIY approach can work if you have strong comps and clean documentation.

How long does a property tax appeal take?

It depends on the jurisdiction. Some informal reviews resolve quickly; formal appeals can take weeks or months, especially if hearings are scheduled in batches. Always assume you’ll need time and file early.

Bottom line

A property tax appeal is a valuation argument, not a complaint about the tax system. If you can show that your assessed value is above market (or the record is wrong), you have a real chance to reduce your tax bill. The keys are strict deadline discipline, strong comps, and simple documentation. If you’re paying taxes through escrow, a successful appeal can also reduce your monthly payment after escrow updates.

Next step: confirm your exemptions, then build a comp set and write a one-page summary. If you can’t find strong comps, don’t waste time—your case may be weak.
