NOI (Net Operating Income) (U.S.): Definition, Formula, and How to Calculate It

NOI is one of the most important metrics in real estate because it isolates the property’s operating performance from the owner’s financing choices. But NOI is also one of the most misunderstood numbers online—especially when people treat it like “profit” or “cash flow.” In this guide you’ll learn what NOI means, how to calculate it step-by-step, what counts as an operating expense, what does not (mortgage payments, depreciation, most CapEx), and how to use NOI to evaluate cap rate, DSCR, and ultimately net rental cash flow.

Quick Answer

NOI (net operating income) is a property’s net income from operations before financing and income taxes. It is calculated as:

NOI = Effective Gross Income − Operating Expenses

NOI is not the same as cash flow because NOI excludes mortgage payments and often excludes CapEx reserves.

What Is NOI?

NOI is designed to answer one core question: How much income does the property generate from operations, independent of how you finance it?

That makes NOI useful because two buyers can purchase the same property with different down payments and different loan terms. Their cash flow might be different, but the property’s operating performance is the same. NOI is the “apples-to-apples” operating number.

Why NOI exists (the point of the metric)

  • Comparability: compare properties without mixing financing choices.
  • Valuation: income properties are often valued as a multiple of NOI (cap rate logic).
  • Lender view: DSCR uses NOI to evaluate debt coverage.
  • Operating improvement: NOI shows whether rent increases or expense cuts actually improve performance.

Think of NOI as: “property engine output” before the owner chooses a transmission (financing).

NOI Formula (With Definitions)

Step 1: Gross Scheduled Income (GSI)

Gross Scheduled Income is the rent you’d collect at full occupancy (plus predictable other income).

Step 2: Vacancy and collection loss

Subtract vacancy and collection loss to get Effective Gross Income (EGI). Vacancy is not optional—NOI uses effective income because it reflects real collection.

Step 3: Operating expenses

Subtract operating expenses to get NOI.

NOI = (Gross Rent + Other Income − Vacancy/Collection Loss) − Operating Expenses

The most common NOI errors happen because people disagree about what counts as “operating expenses.” We’ll break that down next.

How to Calculate NOI (Step-by-Step)

This is the process you can use for a single-family rental, a duplex, or a multi-family property. The structure is the same; the line items change.

Step 1: List all income sources

Start with rent per unit. Add other income only if it’s recurring and collectible: parking, laundry, storage, pet fees, etc. Be conservative with one-time fees.

Step 2: Apply vacancy and loss

Choose a vacancy assumption (percentage or months per year). For single-unit rentals, vacancy is more concentrated risk. For multi-unit, vacancy can be smoother.

Step 3: Compile operating expenses

Operating expenses are the recurring costs to run the property. Verify what you can (taxes, insurance, HOA). Use a realistic maintenance line even if it’s uncertain.

Step 4: Subtract expenses from effective income

The result is NOI. NOI is typically shown annually, but you can compute it monthly too.

Step 5: Sanity-check NOI

Two fast checks: (1) does the expense list look complete, and (2) does your vacancy assumption match reality and turnover?

Tip: If the property is older, maintenance and expense creep are more likely—be conservative.

What Counts as Operating Expenses in NOI?

Operating expenses are costs required to operate and maintain the property’s income-producing ability. In most underwriting, common operating expenses include:

Typical operating expenses

  • Property taxes
  • Insurance
  • Repairs and maintenance (ongoing)
  • Property management
  • Utilities paid by owner (water/sewer/trash, common electric, etc.)
  • HOA / condo fees
  • Landscaping/snow removal
  • Pest control
  • Licenses/permits (if applicable)
  • Advertising/leasing costs (sometimes included here, sometimes in vacancy/turnover bucket)

Why maintenance is tricky

Maintenance is uncertain, which makes it tempting to understate. But understating maintenance inflates NOI and makes the property look more valuable than it is. A conservative placeholder is usually better than a “perfect” guess that’s too low.

Practical rule: If you’re not sure whether an expense belongs, include it in a conservative scenario and test sensitivity.

What Does NOT Count in NOI?

NOI is before financing and income taxes, so these typically do not belong in NOI:

Not included in NOI

  • Mortgage payments (debt service) — principal and interest are financing choices.
  • Depreciation — non-cash accounting, not an operating outflow.
  • Income taxes — depends on the owner’s tax situation.
  • Capital expenditures (CapEx) — often excluded from NOI (but should be modeled separately for cash flow).
  • One-time major improvements — renovations are usually capital items, not operating.

But what about CapEx?

This is where confusion happens. Many NOI definitions exclude CapEx entirely. Some underwriting uses “NOI after reserves” or “adjusted NOI” where a reserve is included. Either approach is fine if clearly labeled, but be consistent.

Best practice: Keep NOI “clean” (operating performance) and then subtract reserves and debt to get cash flow.

NOI vs Cash Flow (Why They’re Different)

NOI is a property-level operating number. Cash flow is an owner-level number. Cash flow depends on financing and reserves.

Convert NOI to cash flow

Cash Flow ≈ NOI − Reserves/CapEx − Debt Service

This matters because many listings quote NOI and make properties look “profitable,” but your cash flow after debt might be thin or negative.

Why NOI is still useful

Even though cash flow matters more for your monthly life, NOI remains essential because it: helps compare properties, supports valuation (cap rate), and feeds DSCR calculations.

NOI and Cap Rate (Valuation Connection)

Cap rate connects NOI to price:

Cap Rate = NOI ÷ Purchase Price (or Value)

Cap rate is a quick way to compare operating yield across properties. But cap rate doesn’t tell you owner cash flow—financing can turn a high-cap-rate property into low cash flow if debt is expensive, and it can turn a low-cap-rate property into acceptable cash flow if debt is cheap and/or down payment is large.

Why inflated NOI inflates value

Because NOI is the numerator in cap rate valuation, overstating NOI can lead to overpaying. If you forget a recurring expense, you’re not just wrong about cash flow—you’re wrong about valuation.

NOI and DSCR (The Lender View)

DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) uses NOI to measure whether the property can cover debt payments:

DSCR = NOI ÷ Annual Debt Service

DSCR is a safety metric. A higher DSCR generally means the property has more margin. Low DSCR means small shocks (vacancy, expense increases) can push the property into negative cash flow.

Insight: A property can have positive cash flow but still be risky if DSCR is thin and reserves are small.

Worked Example: NOI Calculation

Here’s an illustrative example to show the mechanics. The point is structure, not the exact numbers.

Assumptions (monthly): Rent $2,650; other income $0; vacancy 5%; taxes $320; insurance $135; maintenance $175; management 8%; HOA/utilities $80.

Line item Monthly
Gross rent $2,650
Less vacancy (5%) −$133
Effective Gross Income $2,517
Property taxes −$320
Insurance −$135
Maintenance (ongoing) −$175
Management (8%) −$201
HOA/utilities/other −$80
NOI (monthly) $1,606

Annual NOI in this example is roughly $1,606 × 12 = $19,272. But that’s not your cash flow. To estimate cash flow, you’d subtract CapEx reserves and mortgage payments.

Convert to cash flow: Cash Flow ≈ NOI − Reserves/CapEx − Debt Service.

How to Improve NOI

NOI improves when you increase effective income or reduce operating expenses. The most reliable NOI improvements are usually operational rather than “hope-based.”

Increase effective income

  • Raise rent to market (without increasing vacancy excessively)
  • Reduce vacancy through tenant retention and better turnover processes
  • Add legitimate ancillary income (parking, storage, laundry)

Reduce operating expenses

  • Shop insurance and review coverage
  • Preventive maintenance to reduce emergency repairs
  • Vendor optimization and utility savings
  • Challenge incorrect property tax assessments where applicable

Warning: Cutting expenses that reduce tenant quality or maintenance can raise vacancy and hurt NOI long-run.

Common NOI Mistakes

1) Using gross rent instead of effective income

Fix: subtract vacancy and collection loss. NOI uses what you actually collect, not what you wish you collected.

2) Missing recurring operating expenses

Fix: use a checklist and verify taxes, insurance, HOA, and utilities paid by owner.

3) Treating CapEx like an operating expense (or ignoring it entirely)

Fix: keep NOI clean, then model CapEx separately as reserves. Or label your number as “NOI after reserves” if you include it.

4) Mixing owner expenses with property expenses

Fix: NOI should reflect property operations, not the owner’s personal financial choices.

5) Believing NOI equals profit

Fix: remember that financing and reserves exist. NOI is a starting point, not the finish line.

Fast fix: If your NOI feels “too good,” you probably missed vacancy or understated expenses.

Stress Tests (NOI Sensitivity Checks)

1) Vacancy stress test

Increase vacancy. If NOI drops sharply, the property is tenant-demand sensitive.

2) Expense creep test

Increase taxes and insurance. These often rise over time and can compress NOI.

3) Maintenance stress test

Raise maintenance assumptions or add a bad-year repair. NOI that survives only under low maintenance is fragile.

4) Rent growth realism test

Hold rent flat while expenses rise. This tests whether your NOI depends on immediate rent growth.

Quick NOI Checklist

  • ✅ Rent and other income listed
  • ✅ Vacancy/collection loss included
  • ✅ Operating expenses complete (taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, HOA/utilities)
  • ✅ Mortgage payment excluded from NOI
  • ✅ CapEx handled consistently (excluded or labeled if included as reserves)
  • ✅ NOI calculated monthly and annually
  • ✅ Stress tests run (vacancy + taxes/insurance + maintenance)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is NOI (net operating income)?

NOI is a property’s net income from operations before financing and income taxes. It equals effective gross income (income minus vacancy) minus operating expenses.

What is the NOI formula?

NOI = Effective Gross Income − Operating Expenses. Effective Gross Income = Gross Scheduled Income − Vacancy/Collection Loss + Other Income.

Is NOI the same as cash flow?

No. NOI excludes mortgage payments and usually excludes CapEx reserves. Cash flow is what’s left after reserves and debt, so it’s usually lower than NOI for financed properties.

How does NOI relate to cap rate?

Cap rate equals NOI divided by price: Cap Rate = NOI ÷ Purchase Price (or value). It measures operating yield before financing.

Does NOI include property management?

Typically yes—management is an operating expense because it’s a recurring cost of operating the property (even if the current owner self-manages).

Bottom Line

NOI is the property’s operating engine output: effective income minus operating expenses, before financing. It’s essential for comparing properties, understanding cap rate valuation, and measuring debt coverage (DSCR). But NOI is not cash flow. To understand what you’ll actually keep each month, convert NOI into cash flow by subtracting reserves/CapEx and debt service, then stress-test the result with vacancy and expense increases.

Methodology and assumptions

Educational only. NOI definitions can vary by analyst and institution. The key is consistency: use effective income (after vacancy), include recurring operating expenses, keep NOI separate from financing, and model CapEx reserves explicitly when translating NOI to owner cash flow.
