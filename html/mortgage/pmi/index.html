PMI Explained
Cost, How It’s Calculated, and How to Remove It

PMI can be the “hidden” cost that changes affordability when you put less than 20% down. This guide explains what PMI is, how it’s priced, how long you pay it, how to remove it, and when paying PMI might still be a smart trade-off.

What PMI Is (and What It Isn’t)

PMI stands for private mortgage insurance. On many conventional loans, PMI is required when the borrower puts less than 20% down. PMI protects the lender against losses if the borrower defaults.

This is the key misunderstanding: PMI is not “insurance for you.” It doesn’t protect your home, your income, or your equity. It protects the lender — and you pay the premium.

Simple rule: If your down payment is under 20% on a conventional mortgage, assume PMI will be part of your monthly payment until it can be removed.

PMI matters because it affects the true monthly cost of ownership. If you’re comparing rent vs buy or trying to estimate affordability, PMI can be the difference between “works comfortably” and “too tight.”

Why PMI Exists in U.S. Mortgages

Lenders generally view a higher loan-to-value (LTV) loan as riskier. If you put 5–10% down, you have less equity “buffer.” A small decline in home value can leave the lender exposed if they have to foreclose.

PMI is the mechanism that makes high-LTV conventional loans possible at scale. It:

  • helps lenders offer lower down payment loans,
  • reduces default risk exposure, and
  • allows borrowers to buy sooner with less cash.

So PMI is best understood as a trade-off: you pay a premium so you can buy without waiting years to save a 20% down payment.

How PMI Is Calculated

PMI pricing varies by lender and insurer, but it’s often quoted as an annual percentage of the loan amount, then divided into monthly payments. The actual premium depends on multiple risk factors, including:

  • Down payment / LTV (higher LTV generally = higher PMI)
  • Credit profile (stronger credit often = lower PMI)
  • Loan type and structure
  • Loan amount

A helpful way to think about it: PMI is more expensive when the loan is “riskier” for the lender. That’s why the lowest down payment tiers tend to have the highest PMI cost.

Modeling tip: PMI is not a fixed number. If you’re budgeting, run a conservative estimate. If PMI comes in cheaper, that’s upside. If it comes in higher, you’re still safe.

Typical PMI Cost Range (What People Mean by “How Much Is PMI?”)

PMI is often discussed as a percentage of the loan amount per year. But what you care about is the monthly premium. Your PMI could be relatively small or meaningfully large depending on LTV and credit.

Because PMI varies so much, the best approach isn’t guessing the national average — it’s testing a range and seeing whether your decision is sensitive to PMI cost.

PMI feels “small” when

  • You’re close to 20% down (lower LTV)
  • Your credit profile is strong
  • The loan amount is moderate
  • You expect quick equity growth (principal + appreciation)

PMI feels “painful” when

  • You put a very small down payment
  • Credit profile pushes PMI higher
  • Your budget is already tight
  • You expect to move before PMI is removable

The right question is: How long will I be paying PMI, and what does it cost me over that period? That brings us to timeline.

How Long You Pay PMI

PMI isn’t necessarily “forever.” On many conventional loans, it’s meant to be temporary — until your LTV reaches a certain threshold. How long that takes depends on:

  • Down payment (starting LTV)
  • Amortization (principal paydown over time)
  • Home value changes (appreciation can speed up reaching the threshold, decline can slow it)
  • Extra payments (faster paydown can shorten PMI period)

Timeline insight: PMI is most painful when you pay it for years without reaching a removable threshold. If you expect a short hold, PMI can be a bigger issue than people expect.

This is why PMI is connected to amortization: the amortization schedule shows how quickly your balance declines, which influences when you hit LTV targets.

How to Remove PMI

PMI removal depends on your loan type and lender/servicer rules. For many conventional loans, PMI can be removed when the loan reaches a certain LTV threshold and you meet requirements (like a good payment history).

Two practical paths to PMI removal

Path 1: Paydown

  • Make regular payments until the loan balance reaches the target LTV
  • Extra payments can speed this up
  • Often the most straightforward path

Path 2: Value increase (appreciation)

  • If the home’s value rises, your LTV can improve faster
  • You may need an appraisal to document value
  • Works best in strong markets

The right action step is simple: once you think you’re near a removable threshold, contact your servicer, ask what documentation is required, and confirm the steps to remove PMI.

Important: Don’t assume PMI disappears automatically the moment you “feel like” you have 20% equity. PMI removal typically follows specific rules and requires either automatic cancellation conditions or a borrower request.

Strategies to Reduce or Avoid PMI

If PMI is making your payment too high, you have several options — and the best one depends on timeline.

1) Increase down payment (even slightly)

Moving from a very high LTV tier to a lower tier can reduce PMI meaningfully. Sometimes the “last few percent” of down payment reduces PMI more than you’d expect.

2) Consider a different loan structure

Some borrowers use alternative structures (like lender-paid PMI or other options) depending on lender offerings. These can shift cost from “monthly PMI” into a higher interest rate.

3) Plan for removal: pay down faster

If you can make extra principal payments, you may reduce the time you pay PMI. This strategy is especially useful if you’re close to the threshold.

4) Don’t ignore the “buy later” option

If PMI makes ownership unaffordable and your market doesn’t penalize waiting, renting longer while saving more down can be the financially safer move.

Is 20% Down Always the Best Move?

“Just put 20% down” is common advice because it removes PMI and lowers the loan amount. But it’s not always optimal. The real decision is about trade-offs:

  • Monthly payment vs liquidity: More down lowers payment but ties up cash.
  • PMI cost vs time: PMI might be temporary and relatively small.
  • Opportunity cost: Cash used for down payment can’t be used for emergency funds, renovations, or investing.

In a strong income/low-risk scenario, paying PMI for a short period can be fine if it lets you buy a home that fits your timeline and budget. In a tight budget scenario, avoiding PMI can be the difference between stability and stress.

Decision lens: If you’d drain your emergency fund to reach 20% down, PMI may be worth it. A slightly higher payment with healthy cash reserves is often safer than a lower payment with no margin.

How to Model PMI in Your Mortgage Calculator

When you use a mortgage calculator, PMI should not be an afterthought. Here’s a clean way to test it:

Scenario A: Lower down payment (with PMI)

  • Down payment: 5–15%
  • Include PMI in the monthly payment
  • Track how long you’ll likely pay PMI

Scenario B: Higher down payment (no PMI)

  • Down payment: 20%
  • No PMI, lower loan balance
  • But less cash left for reserves/investing

Then compare:

  • Monthly payment difference
  • Total cash required upfront
  • Risk (cash reserves)
  • Timeline to PMI removal

Frequently Asked Questions

What is PMI?

PMI (private mortgage insurance) is insurance that protects the lender on many conventional loans when the borrower puts less than 20% down. It typically increases the monthly payment until it can be removed.

How much does PMI cost per month?

PMI varies by lender and borrower profile. It’s commonly priced as an annual percentage of the loan amount, then paid monthly. The best approach is to estimate PMI in a range and see if your decision is sensitive to it.

When can PMI be removed?

For many conventional loans, PMI can be removed once the loan reaches a certain LTV threshold and you meet requirements. Contact your servicer to confirm the exact rule and whether an appraisal is needed.

Is it always better to put 20% down to avoid PMI?

Not always. Avoiding PMI lowers monthly payment, but a larger down payment ties up cash. The right choice depends on PMI cost, how long you’ll keep the loan, and opportunity cost.

Does PMI apply to all loans?

PMI is common on conventional loans with low down payments. Other loan types may use different forms of mortgage insurance. Always confirm the insurance structure in your Loan Estimate.

Bottom line

PMI is a lender-protection premium that often applies when you put less than 20% down on a conventional mortgage. It can meaningfully increase your monthly payment, but it’s often temporary and removable once your LTV improves. The smart approach is to model PMI as a timeline-based cost and compare it to the liquidity benefits of a lower down payment.
