Rent vs Buy With Property Taxes (U.S.)
Break-Even, True Cost, and How to Model Taxes

Property taxes are one of the biggest “silent” costs in U.S. homeownership — and they can flip a rent vs buy outcome. Taxes don’t build equity, they often rise over time, and they hit your cash flow every year. This guide shows how property taxes affect break-even and how to model taxes realistically over 5, 10, and 30 years.

Why Property Taxes Change Rent vs Buy Results

Property taxes are a recurring cost that doesn’t build equity. That makes them different from principal payments, which become ownership in the home. In rent vs buy terms, property taxes behave like a permanent “service fee” for owning.

Taxes matter so much because they can be large, they can grow, and they apply every year you hold the property. In some markets, property taxes can be similar to (or higher than) the annual maintenance reserve — meaning they meaningfully change your long-run cost profile.

Key point: If you ignore taxes or keep them flat for decades, you can easily produce a misleading “buy wins” result.

Taxes vs Equity: What’s Cost and What Isn’t

A clean rent vs buy model separates:

  • Equity building: principal paydown + appreciation (asset growth)
  • Non-equity costs: interest, taxes, insurance, HOA, maintenance, transaction fees

Property taxes belong in the second group. They reduce your net position without increasing your ownership stake. That’s why they influence break-even: they increase the “carrying cost” of holding a home.

Tax Growth and Reassessment: Don’t Keep Taxes Flat

The most common modeling mistake is taking year-one property taxes and applying that same dollar amount forever. In reality, taxes can change as assessments change, local rates change, or reassessments occur after a purchase. How taxes behave varies by jurisdiction — but the modeling principle is the same: use a growth assumption and stress-test it.

How to model taxes realistically

  • Base case: start with the best estimate of current annual tax and grow it modestly over time.
  • Conservative scenario: test a higher tax growth rate.
  • Reassessment risk: run a scenario where taxes jump early, then grow from that new base.

Practical test: If a “tax jump” scenario flips the outcome, your decision is sensitive to tax uncertainty — which is especially important for 3–10 year horizons.

Escrow: Why Your Monthly Payment Can Jump

Many U.S. mortgages include escrow: the lender collects money each month for property taxes and insurance, then pays those bills when due. This is convenient, but it can create surprises:

  • If taxes rise, your escrow payment can increase.
  • If insurance rises, escrow can increase.
  • If escrow was underfunded, you can owe a shortage and see a higher monthly payment.

In rent vs buy modeling, escrow doesn’t change the total cost — it changes cash flow timing. But cash flow is real. If ownership costs already stretch your budget, escrow volatility matters.

Cash flow rule: If you can only afford buying when escrow stays perfect, the plan is fragile.

Why Taxes Matter More Over 3–10 Years

Over 30 years, equity building can be large enough that taxes feel “small” in comparison, depending on appreciation and your mortgage structure. Over 3–10 years, equity building is smaller and selling costs are large — so recurring costs like taxes can dominate.

Short holds (3–5 years)

  • Taxes are a large share of the timeline
  • Equity build-up is limited early
  • Selling costs can wipe out gains
  • Tax increases can flip results fast

Long holds (10–30 years)

  • More time for equity growth
  • Costs get “diluted” across more years
  • Taxes still matter, but are less likely to dominate alone

If you might move soon, pair this guide with 3–5 years reality check.

Tax Benefits: How to Model Safely (Without Overpromising)

Some buyers assume large tax deductions will make owning dramatically cheaper. Sometimes tax benefits matter — but they depend on your overall tax situation and aren’t guaranteed. A safe modeling approach is to treat tax benefits as a separate scenario, not the baseline that your decision depends on.

Safe approach

  • Baseline: run the comparison without assuming large tax benefits.
  • Tax benefit scenario: add estimated benefits and see how much the result changes.
  • Decision quality: if buying only works with aggressive tax assumptions, treat it as fragile.

Principle: Don’t require tax benefits for the purchase to “work.” Treat them as upside if they happen, not the foundation of the decision.

Property Tax Checklist (Quick)

Before you finalize your model, do a quick sanity check:

Numbers

  • Current annual tax estimate (best available)
  • Whether taxes recently increased in the area
  • Whether reassessment after purchase is common
  • Plan for growth instead of flat taxes

Cash flow

  • Escrow included?
  • Can your budget handle an escrow increase?
  • Are taxes + HOA a large share of monthly cost?
  • Do you have reserves for volatility?

Best practice: If you’re unsure, model taxes conservatively. Underestimating taxes is one of the most common ways to make buying look too attractive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do high property taxes make renting better than buying?

High property taxes increase the non-equity cost of owning and can push break-even farther out, especially over 3–10 years. They don’t automatically mean renting is better, but taxes must be modeled realistically (including growth and reassessments) to compare net position fairly.

How should I model property taxes over 10 to 30 years?

Avoid keeping taxes flat. Model taxes with a realistic growth assumption and consider reassessment behavior. Run conservative scenarios where taxes rise faster than expected to see how sensitive your outcome is.

Do tax deductions change rent vs buy outcomes?

Sometimes, but they depend on your tax situation. It’s safer to run a baseline scenario without assuming large deductions and then run a separate scenario with estimated tax benefits.

Why do property taxes matter more for short holding periods?

Because taxes are a recurring cash cost that doesn’t build equity. Over short horizons, taxes take a larger share of your timeline and can overwhelm the modest equity you build early, especially after selling costs.

What’s the fastest “tax reality check” before buying?

Run a “tax jump + growth” scenario and see if buying still wins over your true holding period. If taxes flip the outcome, your decision is sensitive — and renting may be the safer financial strategy.

Bottom Line: Taxes Are a Permanent Cost — Model Them Like It

Property taxes can be a decisive factor in rent vs buy because they reduce net position without building equity. They also tend to change over time, making “flat tax forever” models misleading.

Run conservative scenarios: taxes rising faster, a possible reassessment jump, and realistic selling costs. If buying still wins, it’s stronger. If it only wins under optimistic tax assumptions, treat buying as a fragile decision — and consider renting for flexibility.
